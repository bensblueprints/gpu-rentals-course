Foundations

Three memories. Three speeds.
Confuse them and you waste money.

VRAM (on the GPU)

The GPU's private workspace. Everything that matters happens here. If your model doesn't fit in VRAM, it crawls or dies. This is the number you shop by: 16GB, 24GB, 32GB, 80GB.

RAM (system)

Staging area. Holds the dataset, the OS, and models while they load into VRAM. You need 2× your VRAM in RAM — cheap, so don't skimp.

Disk (NVMe)

Cold storage for models and output. A 7GB model loads at ~1-3 GB/s on NVMe. Slow disk = 2-minute startup every time the model reloads.

Rule of thumb: VRAM decides if it works. Bandwidth decides how fast. Disk decides how often you wait.

Use the kitchen analogy: VRAM is the counter you cook on, RAM is the fridge, disk is the pantry in the basement. A chef with a tiny counter can't cook a big meal no matter how big the pantry is.

Common beginner mistake to call out: shopping by "GPU name" (4090! H100!) instead of asking "does my model fit in this card's VRAM?"

The 2× RAM rule catches people: rental listings with 24GB VRAM and 16GB system RAM exist. Loading a big model then feels broken. Check it before renting.

Foundations

VRAM is a bouncer, not a speedometer

  • Model weights must fit. An 8B LLM ≈ 5GB. A 14B ≈ 10GB (quantized). SDXL + LoRA ≈ 12GB. Video models ≈ 24-40GB.
  • Speed comes from bandwidth, not size. A 5090 (32GB, 1,800 GB/s) beats an A100 80GB (1,600 GB/s) at image work — even though it's "smaller."
  • Too little VRAM = offloading. The GPU shuffles data from RAM every step. 5× slower. This is why a 16GB card can't run a 32B LLM properly.
  • KV cache eats VRAM on LLMs. Long conversations need room beyond the model weights. A 24GB card = 14B model + comfortable cache.

The bouncer line lands well: VRAM doesn't decide how fast the party is — it decides who gets in. If the model doesn't fit, nothing else about the card matters.

Walk the 5090-vs-A100 example slowly. Everyone assumes "80GB datacenter card beats 32GB consumer card." For a 12GB image workload, the A100's extra 68GB sits empty while its lower bandwidth loses the race. Bigger ≠ faster.

Offloading: some tools (llama.cpp, ComfyUI low-VRAM mode) will "make it fit" by paging layers through RAM. It works — at 5× slower. Fine for a demo, fatal for production.

Foundations

Quantization: how a 14B model fits in 10GB

  • Weights are stored as numbers. 16-bit (FP16): 14B params ≈ 28GB. 8-bit: ~14GB. 4-bit (AWQ/GPTQ): ~10GB.
  • 4-bit is the sweet spot for serving. Quality loss on story/chat workloads is small; the VRAM savings decide which card you rent.
  • Below 4-bit, quality falls off a cliff for nuance-heavy writing. Test before you ship on 3-bit.
  • KV cache is the hidden second tenant. Every active conversation keeps its context in VRAM. 20 concurrent players × long chats can eat as much as the model itself.

Shopping rule: model GB + cache headroom ≤ VRAM. That inequality picks your card.

Analogy: quantization is compressing a photo from RAW to JPEG. At 4-bit you're at "high-quality JPEG" — nobody playing a text game can tell. At 2-bit you're deep-fried.

The KV cache point is the one that bites people in production: the model fit on the card in testing, then real users with long story histories OOM it. Leave 30-40% headroom for serving, not 5%.

Vocabulary they'll see in listings: AWQ, GPTQ, GGUF, EXL2 — all quantization formats. For vLLM serving: AWQ. For local single-user: GGUF.

Inference is bandwidth-bound: the GPU spends its life re-reading model weights. Training is compute-bound. That's why the same card ranks differently per job.

This is the slide that stops people over-buying. Inference re-reads the whole model for every token it generates — so the speed of memory, not the size of the compute engine, sets the pace.

Punchline: a card can be the wrong champion for your job while being a great card. Match the bottleneck, not the badge.

The Hidden Bottleneck

Transfer speed kills more jobs
than weak GPUs ever will

  • Network varies 100× between hosts. I've seen 60 Mbps and 6,783 Mbps on the same platform. A 53GB transfer is 2 minutes on one and 2 hours on the other.
  • Region matters for inter-instance transfers. Cross-continent = 1 MB/s. Same region = 50+ MB/s. Move data host-to-host, never through your home connection.
  • PCIe barely matters for inference. x8 vs x16? Invisible for image gen. Matters for multi-GPU training only.
  • Check before you rent: network Mbps, reliability %, region. Not just the GPU name.

Story to tell: the 53GB dataset story is real. Same platform, two hosts, same price — one finished the transfer in 2 minutes, the other quoted 2 hours. The GPU was identical. The job died on the wire, not the silicon.

The home-connection trap: people download 50GB to their laptop "to be safe," then upload it to the next host. Your home uplink turns a 2-minute job into a weekend. Host-to-host, always.

Practical tip: on arrival, run a quick speedtest/curl before loading anything. Bad network? Kill the instance in the first 5 minutes while it costs pennies.

The Hidden Bottleneck

The data movement playbook

Rule 1: data lives near compute

Pick the host's region first, then the GPU. Your dataset's region is your region. Cross-continent transfers are a tax on every job.

Rule 2: seed once, reuse everywhere

Keep a "golden" storage volume or cloud bucket with models + datasets. New hosts pull from it at datacenter speed — not from your house.

Rule 3: compress what you sync

rsync -z, or tarball model outputs before moving. 30k PNGs move faster as a stream of archives than 30k individual files.

Rule 4: verify on arrival

curl huggingface.co, quick speedtest, disk space check. 60 seconds of checks beats discovering a dead network at hour 3.

Frame it as plumbing before racing: nobody brags about their pipes, but every 3am failure is a plumbing failure.

Rule 2 is the biggest quality-of-life unlock: a persistent volume or bucket with your base models means a new host is productive in minutes, and you never re-download a 7GB model from HuggingFace per instance.

Rule 3 catches people with image pipelines: 30k small files = 30k round trips. Tar them per batch; transfers go from hours to minutes.

The Cheat Sheet

The 60-second listing check
(do this before every rental)

  • 1. VRAM ≥ model + headroom. The inequality from earlier. Non-negotiable.
  • 2. Reliability ≥ 98%. Below that, expect the host to die mid-job.
  • 3. Network down/up listed in Mbps. No number shown = assume it's bad.
  • 4. Region matches your data. Same region as your volume/bucket.
  • 5. RAM ≥ 2× VRAM, disk ≥ dataset + outputs. Cheap lines on the listing, expensive to get wrong.
  • 6. Price vs the fleet alternative. Would N cheaper cards beat this one for this job?

Sixty seconds of checks. Zero 3am surprises.

Run this live if teaching in person: open a real marketplace, pick a listing, read it against the checklist out loud. Students copy behavior, not bullet points.

#3 is the one everyone skips. Listings proudly show the GPU name in 40pt type and hide the network in fine print — which is exactly backwards from what kills jobs.

#6 is a habit, not a calculation: before clicking "rent" on anything over $0.50/hr, ask the fleet question once.

Use Case · LLMs

Running LLMs: vLLM changes everything

  • 8B model (Llama 3.1): fits a 16GB card. Fine for dev, weak for production stories.
  • 14B 4-bit (Qwen 2.5 AWQ): ~10GB. The price-performance king — a 3090 at $0.25/hr serves hundreds of concurrent players.
  • 32B 4-bit: ~20GB. Needs a 32GB card. Marginal quality gain over 14B in games.
  • vLLM batches requests — one 3090 handles 20-40 simultaneous users before anyone notices. Your card is mostly idle waiting for reads.

Real math: $0.25/hr serves 1,000+ active players.

Explain why batching is magic: without vLLM, each request runs alone and the GPU idles between tokens. With continuous batching, vLLM interleaves 20-40 requests through the same weights — throughput goes up 10×+ on the same card.

Why 14B is the king: story quality plateaus fast for game narration, but price doesn't. A 3090 serving 14B-AWQ is the cheapest "players can't tell the difference" point on the curve.

The 1,000+ players math: most players read far more than they type. A game request every ~30s per active player × vLLM batching = one card absorbs a crowd. Cost per player rounds to zero.

Use Case · Images

Image generation: scale out, not up

  • 8× 4090 beats 1× H100 for batch generation — and costs less per image.

Real numbers: 30,000 scenes in 5 hours for ~$27.

Use Case · Images

Fleet design: the LoRA-hot queue

  • The problem: loading SDXL + a LoRA takes 30-60s. Naive round-robin reloads models constantly — your fleet spends its life loading, not generating.
  • The fix: pin jobs to workers by model. Worker 3 runs character A jobs all night. The model loads once, stays hot, generates back-to-back.
  • The queue: one folder/database of pending jobs per game. Workers pull the next job matching their loaded LoRA, write output + metadata, mark done.
  • The result: 3× throughput on identical hardware. Zero new spend — just routing.

Architecture in one line: one worker per GPU, one LoRA per worker, one queue per game.

This is the highest-ROI slide in the deck for anyone doing batch images. Same cards, same models, 3× output — purely from not reloading weights.

Draw it on a whiteboard if available: queue → router → 8 workers, each labeled with its resident LoRA. Students should see that "routing" is just "check which model the job needs, send it to the worker that has it."

Failure mode to mention: a worker whose queue empties should fall back to loading the next-most-demanded LoRA — otherwise one idle card wastes the night while others queue up.

Use Case · Training

Training LoRAs: VRAM is the wall

  • SDXL LoRA training: 24GB works, 40GB is comfortable, 80GB is luxurious.
  • 8× A100 40GB trained 246 character LoRAs at ~21 min each, 16/hour across the fleet.
  • Consumer cards (3090/4090) train fine at 24GB — just slower per job.
  • Training is a burst job. Rent big, finish, destroy. Never own training hardware unless you train weekly.

Why training eats VRAM when inference doesn't: training holds the model + optimizer states + gradients + the batch, all at once. Optimizer states alone can be 2× the model size. That's why the same SDXL that serves in 12GB wants 24-40GB to train.

The 246-LoRA run is the proof of the fleet pattern: no single A100 is fast — 21 min per LoRA — but eight of them in parallel is a factory. Total wall-clock for 246 models: about 15 hours, not 86.

"Rent big, finish, destroy": a weekend of 8× A100s costs less than the electricity a owned rig burns in a month of sitting idle. Training hardware you own is a gym membership you feel guilty about.

Use Case · Training

The 21-minute LoRA: what's inside

Dataset

20-40 generated images of the character, varied angles/expressions/lighting. Consistency of identity, variety of everything else. Captions describe the variable parts, never the identity.

Hyperparameters

SDXL, ~1,500-2,500 steps, batch 1-4, 4-bit/base FP16. Rank 16-32 is plenty for a face. More rank ≠ more likeness — it's more overfitting.

Time math

~21 min on an A100 40GB. ~35-45 min on a 4090. Cost per LoRA: well under $1 either way.

Quality gate

Every LoRA generates a fixed test grid (same seed, same prompts) before joining the fleet. Bad LoRAs get retrained, not shipped.

The asset isn't the GPU time — it's the repeatable recipe. Write yours down once; every future character is a rerun.

Captioning is where beginners fail: whatever you write in the caption, the LoRA learns is "not the character." Caption "smiling, rainy street" — never caption the scar, the jawline, the hair. Those must stay attached to the trigger token.

The test-grid gate matters more as the fleet grows: with 246 LoRAs, a 5% dud rate is 12 broken characters silently poisoning your game. Automate the check.

If asked "why not fine-tune the whole model?": a full SDXL fine-tune wants 80GB+, hours per model, and produces a 7GB artifact per character. LoRAs are ~100-300MB each and stack/swizzle at runtime. For 246 characters, it's the only sane option.

Use Case · Video

Video generation: the honest picture

  • The VRAM floor is real. LTX-Video / Wan-class models want 24GB minimum, 32-80GB for comfortable quality and length.
  • Architecture matters as much as size. Newer video models get more seconds-per-GB than last year's at the same VRAM — check model requirements, not folklore.
  • Throughput is the constraint. A 5s clip ≈ minutes of GPU time. Budget video = batch fleets again, not one monster card.
  • Cost reality: a clip on a rented 5090 via ComfyUI lands at ~$0.02-0.05 — if your pipeline is tuned. Untuned, it's 10× that.

Same playbook as images: queue, batch, fleet — then tune the pipeline before you touch the hardware budget.

Set expectations: video is where the marketing demos and the rental bill diverge hardest. Everyone sees the 5-second demo clip; nobody shows the 4 minutes of compute behind it.

The tuning levers, in order of impact: resolution (drops quadratically), frame count, steps, then model choice. A 480p→720p decision can double your bill for a difference nobody sees on a phone screen.

Connect to the ads pipeline if relevant: the NightTale/Questner-style 8s ad scenes are exactly this workload — one clip, one style, batched across a fleet.

Use Case · Voice

TTS and voice cloning: the cheap seat

  • Small models, small cards. Modern TTS runs on 8GB, some on CPU. This is the one workload where the GPU is almost an afterthought.
  • Latency is the real spec. For games, voice that arrives 5s after the text feels broken. Target real-time-factor < 0.3 and stream the audio.
  • Voice cloning = one-time cost. Clone the narrator once, serve forever. Store the voice model with your other assets.
  • Don't rent a dedicated box for this. Colocate TTS on the same instance as your LLM — they idle at different times.

Quick slide — the takeaway is don't overspend here. Students who just learned "GPUs are the bottleneck" need the counterexample: voice is cheap, small, and loves colocation.

The latency point is the only real engineering: generate sentence-by-sentence and stream, rather than rendering the whole paragraph. Perceived latency drops 5× with the same model.

Brand-VO example if teaching with the ads material: one cloned narrator voice reads every end card — consistency across 15 games, zero recurring cost.

Operations

Batch jobs that don't die at 3am

Structure

One worker per GPU, one game/dataset per worker. Simple resumable jobs: skip what's already done, write metadata as you go, log everything.

Watchdog

A tiny script that checks: one master alive, no duplicate workers, GPU util healthy. Restart what dies. My fleet self-heals via cron every 15 min.

Lock files

flock -n prevents duplicate masters. I once ran 3 trainers on 4 GPUs at ⅓ speed for hours. Never again.

Sync out continuously

rsync results home every 60s. If the host dies (it will), you lose seconds, not hours.

Frame the whole section: community-cloud pricing buys you unreliability. Operations is how you keep the discount anyway. None of this is clever — it's four boring habits.

Resumability is the keystone: if "rerun the script" is always safe, then every failure becomes a speed bump. Design jobs so that running them twice produces the same result as running them once.

The 3-trainers story is worth the laugh: three processes sharing GPUs, each getting a third of the memory bandwidth, all "working," none failing. Utilization was 100%. Throughput was 33%.

Operations

The entire reliability stack, in code

# 1. One master, ever — the script refuses to double-run
exec 9>/tmp/master.lock
flock -n 9 || { echo "already running"; exit 1; }

# 2. Workers: skip finished work, log everything
for job in pending_jobs(); do
  [ -f "out/$job.done" ] && continue   # resumable by design
  run_job "$job" && touch "out/$job.done"
done

# 3. Watchdog, via cron every 15 min — revive what's dead
*/15 * * * *  pgrep -f master.sh >/dev/null || /opt/fleet/master.sh

# 4. Sync out every 60s — hosts die, results don't
while true; do rsync -az out/ home:results/; sleep 60; done

That's it. No orchestrator, no Kubernetes, no framework. Four patterns you can read in one screen.

Read each block aloud and name the failure it kills: block 1 kills duplicate masters, block 2 kills lost progress, block 3 kills silent death, block 4 kills lost output.

Resist the urge to impress with infrastructure: the fleet that trained 246 LoRAs ran on shell scripts and cron. Boring tools fail in boring, debuggable ways at 3am.

If students ask about scaling past ~10 hosts: the same four patterns survive — the queue moves to a database, the watchdog gains a dashboard. The philosophy doesn't change.

Operations

Hosts die. Plan for it.

  • Reliability % is the most important number on the listing. 93% means your 8-hour job dies at hour 5. 99.9% costs 10% more and saves the whole job.
  • Kill-and-retry is a feature, not a failure. Every long job I run is resumable by design. New host, rerun the script, it skips finished work.
  • Provisioning hangs happen. If a template download stalls 30+ minutes, kill the download process — provisioning usually completes anyway.
  • Some hosts can't reach HuggingFace. Test on arrival: curl huggingface.co. No reach? Move the data from a host that can.

Make reliability % concrete: 93% sounds like an A-minus. It's not — it's a coin flip on whether tonight's job finishes. Uptime compounds across hours and across an 8-host fleet: one weak host drags the whole fleet's effective uptime.

"Kill-and-retry is a feature": flip the emotional framing. On community cloud, a dead host isn't bad luck — it's the expected tax, already priced into the 2-3× discount. Resumability means you pay it in minutes.

The HuggingFace trick is a real one-liner worth memorizing. Some hosts' networks block or throttle it; discovering that after loading a dataset costs you the evening.

Asset Discipline

What you keep forever
(it's never the hardware)

  • Models and LoRAs — your trained assets. Three copies: local, NAS, cloud. Hardware dies; these are the business.
  • Pipelines as code — every script, every config, every watchdog. Rebuild any host in an hour.
  • Metadata — which prompt made which image. Regeneration without it means starting over.
  • Nothing on the instance. Treat every host as temporary. If it matters, it's synced off within 60 seconds.

The mental model: you're not renting computers, you're renting electricity with a return policy. Everything that outlives the rental must leave the machine.

Metadata is the one people skip and regret: 30,000 images without the prompt/seed/LoRA that made them is 30,000 orphans. Store it next to every output, every time.

Test your discipline: "if this host vanished right now, what would I lose?" The correct answer is "up to 60 seconds of output." Anything more is a design flaw, not bad luck.

Operations

Rented-box hygiene

  • Assume the host is hostile and the neighbors are curious. Community hardware = someone else's machine. No personal SSH keys with broad access; use a throwaway keypair per project.
  • API keys live in env vars or secret files you sync in — never baked into images, scripts, or git repos pushed to the host.
  • Encrypt archives in transit to untrusted storage if the content matters (gpg -c or an encrypted bucket). Your LoRAs are your moat; treat them like it.
  • Wipe on departure. Secure-delete datasets and outputs before destroying the instance. The next renter gets the disk.

Keep this practical, not paranoid: the goal isn't a fortress, it's not being the easy target. Four habits, two minutes each.

The throwaway keypair habit is the one to drill: a key that only unlocks this project's hosts, deleted when the project ends. If a host is compromised, the blast radius is one project, not your life.

"The next renter gets the disk" lands viscerally — mention that deleted files on a returned instance are often recoverable. Wipe what matters.
